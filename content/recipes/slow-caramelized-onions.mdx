## Overview

A simple exercise in patience and heat control: cook one very large yellow onion until its sharp, watery character gives way to a soft, concentrated, sweet-savory flavor. The finished onions can top steak or grilled cheese, enrich pasta, or become the base for French onion soup.

**Cooking record — November 20, 2026:** I bought a giant onion specifically to practice caramelization, then used the cooked onions to make a rustic French onion soup with beef stock and thyme. The soup initially tasted too strongly of beef stock but developed a sweet-salty balance as it simmered. I enjoyed it, though I wanted a more prominent onion flavor. That is feedback on the *finished soup*, not a separate tasting review of the onions alone.

> **Recipe fidelity:** The process below reconstructs the technique discussed and practiced. Exact amounts of butter/oil, total caramelization time, pan material, and final onion color were not confirmed. These are repeatable working quantities, not documented measurements from the cook.

## Equipment

- Large, heavy-bottomed skillet (stainless steel makes browning easier to see; cast iron also works)
- Sharp knife and cutting board
- Wooden spoon or heatproof spatula
- Small cup of water for deglazing

## Ingredients

- 1 very large yellow onion (approximately 350–450 g before peeling)
- 1 tbsp unsalted butter
- 1 tbsp olive oil or avocado oil
- 1/4 tsp kosher salt to start, or to taste
- 2–6 tbsp water, added in small splashes as needed (not all at once)

*The amounts above are a reproducible starting point. A later soup recipe also discussed using 2 tbsp butter without added oil.*

## Instructions

1. **Slice evenly.** Trim and peel the onion. Halve from stem to root, then slice into approximately 1/8-inch half-moons, preferably pole-to-pole so the strands hold together during the long cook.
2. **Start the onions.** Set a wide skillet over medium heat. Melt the butter with the oil, then add the onion and a small pinch of salt. Toss until coated.
3. **Sweat and soften.** Cook for roughly 8–12 minutes, stirring periodically. The onion will release water, turn translucent, and shrink substantially. At this stage, focus on softening rather than aggressive browning.
4. **Lower the heat as needed.** Once the pan is less wet and the onion begins turning gold, move toward medium-low. Stir every few minutes, more frequently as browning accelerates.
5. **Manage the fond.** If brown residue builds on the pan before the onions are ready, add 1–2 tbsp water and scrape the flavorful browned bits into the onions. Let the water evaporate before continuing. Repeat as needed; don't allow the fond to turn black.
6. **Cook to deep amber.** Continue until the onions are very soft, glossy, substantially reduced, and deep golden to chestnut brown. A very large onion may take **45–75 minutes total**, sometimes longer depending on pan size, heat, and moisture.
7. **Taste and finish.** Taste a strand. It should be sweet, savory, and mellow, without raw onion bite or bitter burned notes. Adjust salt only after tasting, especially if the onions will go into a salty soup or sauce.

## What to Look For

| Stage | Visual cue | Flavor and texture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sweating | Pale, glossy, reduced in volume | Softening, still recognizably onion-like |
| Early browning | Light golden edges | Sharper notes fading; sweetness emerging |
| Mid-browning | Amber strands and brown fond | Richer, sweeter, increasingly savory |
| Fully caramelized | Deep brown, glossy, jammy | Concentrated, mellow, complex |

These stages are more useful than a stopwatch. Avoid confusing **deep brown** with **blackened**.

## Why It Works

Onions begin with considerable water. Slow cooking evaporates that moisture and concentrates naturally occurring sugars and other flavor compounds. As the pan dries, browning reactions—including Maillard chemistry and sugar-related thermal reactions—build the deep, roasted, sweet-savory character associated with caramelized onions.

**Deglazing** is a control tool: a little water dissolves browned fond and briefly cools the pan, helping preserve flavor before it burns. It doesn't undo genuinely burned, bitter material.

The technique rewards restraint. Sugar and baking soda can change the speed or flavor, but neither is needed for this foundational version.

## Ways to Use Them

- **Rustic French onion soup:** Add beef stock and thyme, simmer, and serve with toasted bread.
- **Steak:** Spoon over seared beef, optionally brightened with a little Dijon or vinegar.
- **Pasta:** Fold into rigatoni with Parmesan, butter, and a splash of pasta water.
- **Grilled cheese:** Pair with sharp cheddar or Gruyère on toasted bread.
- **Mashed potatoes:** Swirl into pommes purée for a sweet-savory variation.

## Next Time

> **Ideas for future testing, not changes documented from this cook.**

- Weigh the raw onion and finished yield to understand moisture loss.
- Photograph the onions at 10-minute intervals and note the burner setting, pan, and final cook time.
- Taste at pale, golden, and deep-brown stages to isolate how flavor changes.
- For a more onion-forward French onion soup, test **two large onions with the same 2 cups of beef stock** instead of simply reducing a beef-heavy broth. Reduction concentrates the stock too.
- Try a tiny amount of vinegar on a separate finished portion to observe how acidity balances sweetness.

## Storage

Cool promptly and refrigerate in a sealed container. Use within 3–4 days, or freeze in small portions for later sauces and soups.
