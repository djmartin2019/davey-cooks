## Overview

A pantry-friendly version of French onion soup, made with one very large caramelized yellow onion, beef stock, thyme, bread, and a sprinkle of Parmesan. This version skips the usual wine and Gruyère topping, allowing the onions and stock to carry the dish.

**Cooking record — November 20, 2026:** I caramelized a giant onion as a technique exercise and turned it into soup with beef stock and thyme. At first, the beef stock tasted too dominant. As the soup simmered, I began tasting a sweet-and-salty balance that I enjoyed. I still wanted a little more onion flavor.

> **Recipe fidelity:** The onion, beef stock, thyme, bread, and Parmesan were all used in this cook, and the soup was cooked and tasted. The quantities below reflect the working recipe discussed, not independently measured amounts from the finished dish. Toasting the bread was part of the plan, but how it was prepared was not recorded.

## Equipment

- Large skillet or heavy-bottomed saucepan
- Wooden spoon or heatproof spatula
- Knife and cutting board
- Ladle and soup bowls
- Toaster, skillet, or oven for the bread

## Ingredients

**Working recipe — approximately 1 generous or 2 smaller servings:**

- 1 very large yellow onion (approximately 350–450 g before peeling)
- 2 tbsp unsalted butter (working amount; exact fat used was not recorded)
- 2 cups (475 ml) beef stock
- 2–3 sprigs fresh thyme
- 1–2 slices bread, preferably sturdy enough to toast well
- Grated Parmesan, for sprinkling (amount not recorded)
- Salt, to taste, added cautiously if the stock is already seasoned
- Freshly ground black pepper, to taste
- A few tablespoons water, as needed while caramelizing the onion

**Optional finishing adjustments, not confirmed as used:** A few drops of vinegar for brightness, or a dash of Worcestershire sauce for savory depth. These are alternatives, not part of the verified cook.

## Instructions

1. **Slice the onion.** Peel the onion, halve it from root to stem, and slice into fairly even, thin half-moons (about 1/8 inch). Uniform pieces soften and brown more consistently.
2. **Start caramelizing.** Melt the butter in a large skillet over medium heat. Add the onions and a small pinch of salt. Cook, stirring occasionally, until softened and reduced substantially in volume, about 10–15 minutes.
3. **Develop deep color.** Reduce the heat toward medium-low as the moisture evaporates. Continue cooking until the onions are deeply golden to chestnut brown, soft, and jammy. This can take **45–75 minutes total** for a giant onion. Stir more frequently as they darken. If the fond starts getting too dark or sticks aggressively, add 1–2 tbsp water, scrape it into the onions, and let the water evaporate. See [Slow-Caramelized Onions](/recipes/slow-caramelized-onions) for the full technique.
4. **Deglaze with stock.** Pour in approximately 1/4 cup of the beef stock and scrape up the browned fond. Add the remaining stock and the thyme sprigs.
5. **Simmer gently.** Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for roughly 15–25 minutes, tasting as the flavors combine. Leave uncovered if the broth is weak and needs concentration; partially cover if the broth is already strongly flavored or salty and you want to limit evaporation.
6. **Taste and balance.** Taste a spoonful containing both broth **and** onion strands. Add black pepper and only as much salt as needed. If the soup is too concentrated, add a little water. If it seems heavy or overly sweet, a few drops of vinegar can add contrast, but won't create more onion flavor.
7. **Toast the bread.** Toast 1–2 slices until golden and quite crisp. This can be done in a toaster, skillet, or a 400°F oven for approximately 5–8 minutes, depending on thickness. The crisp texture helps the bread withstand the broth.
8. **Serve.** Remove the thyme stems, ladle the soup into bowls, add the bread, and sprinkle with Parmesan. The traditional broiled Gruyère crust is intentionally omitted from this pantry version.

## Kitchen Notes

### What Worked

- **The soup improved as it simmered.** My first impression was that the beef stock overwhelmed the onions. Later tastes became sweeter and saltier, with a balance I genuinely liked.
- **The onion preparation paid off.** Slow caramelization gave the simple soup a sweet-savory foundation without requiring wine, Gruyère, or a long ingredient list.
- **Tasting during cooking changed the diagnosis.** Rather than immediately changing the recipe after the first taste, I let it continue cooking and noticed how the overall flavor evolved.

### What I'd Improve

- **More onion presence.** The finished soup was enjoyable, but I wanted the onion flavor to be slightly more prominent relative to the beef stock.
- **Reconsider the ratio.** For the next batch, try **two large onions with the same 2 cups of beef stock**, rather than reducing the existing stock further.

## Why It Works

**Caramelized onions supply the base.** Long, gentle cooking drives off moisture and develops a concentrated mix of sweetness, savory depth, and browned aromas. The onion's transformation matters more than adding lots of seasonings.

**Beef stock adds body and savory intensity.** Stock can also overwhelm a small quantity of onions, especially when it is strongly flavored or salty. More stock does not automatically make a better onion soup.

**Simmering integrates flavors; reduction concentrates them.** Simmering allows onion flavor to move into the broth. Simmering *uncovered* also evaporates water, concentrating both onion and beef flavors. If the beef is already too prominent, reduction alone is not a targeted fix; it may make the soup beefier and saltier.

**Thyme provides aromatic contrast.** Its herbal character helps keep the broth from tasting only sweet and meaty.

**Acidity changes perception, not ingredient ratios.** A small amount of vinegar can make a rich soup taste brighter, but it cannot replace the flavor contribution of additional onions.

## Next Time

> **Future experiments, not changes verified in this cook.**

1. **Double the onions, keep the stock fixed:** Use two large onions and 2 cups beef stock to test whether the soup becomes more onion-forward without excessive sweetness.
2. **Record the stock:** Note its brand, sodium content, and how it tastes before adding it. A lower-sodium stock may give more control over reduction.
3. **Compare covered versus uncovered simmering:** Taste equal portions before and after reduction to distinguish stronger overall flavor from better onion-to-beef balance.
4. **Try a little acid at the end:** Add a few drops of sherry vinegar or another mild vinegar to a small portion and compare with the unadjusted soup.
5. **Try the classic gratinée finish:** When ingredients are available, top toasted bread with Gruyère and broil in an oven-safe bowl. This is a separate variation, not what was documented for this cook.
6. **Photograph each stage:** Capture the final onions, broth after stock addition, and plated soup for a stronger cooking journal entry.

## Storage

Cool the soup promptly and refrigerate in a sealed container for **3–4 days**. Keep toasted bread separate so it does not become soggy. Reheat the soup until steaming hot; toast fresh bread just before serving.
